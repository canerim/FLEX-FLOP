\section{Architecture details}

\paragraph{Costs.} With $K$ exits over $N{=}12$ trunk blocks and split depth $j$,
the cost of exit $k$ in units of one released decode is
\begin{equation}
c_k = s_{\mathrm{up}} + s_{\mathrm{trunk}}\frac{(k{+}1)b}{N}
      + s_{\mathrm{head}} + a_k + r,
\end{equation}
where $b = N/K$ blocks per exit, $s_{\mathrm{up}} = 0.0816$,
$s_{\mathrm{trunk}} = 0.8944$, $s_{\mathrm{head}} = 0.0240$ are the measured
shares of the released decoder, $a_k$ is the adapter's share and $r$ the
seam-repair module's $0.0095$. Only the trunk term depends on $k$: the stem and
head run for every tile at every depth, which is why the ceiling
$100(1-c_j)$ is well below $100$ however shallow the ladder gets.

\paragraph{Adapters.} $\mathrm{Ad}(f) = f + Wf$ with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}$
zero-initialised costs $C^2$ MAC/px, one eighth of a block's $8C^2 + 9C$. The
FFN variant, $\mathrm{Ad}(f)=f+\mathrm{PW}(\mathrm{WSiLUChunkAdd}
(\mathrm{PW}_{C\to 4C}(f)))$, costs $2C^2$. Assignment is by the number of blocks
the exit skips: FFN at four or more, the $1\times1$ otherwise.

\section{The tiling penalty in detail}

\paragraph{Why only the depthwise matters.} A \texttt{DepthConvBlock} at $C=384$
costs $8C^2 + 9C = 1{,}183{,}104$ MAC/px, of which the $3\times3$ depthwise is
$9C = 3{,}456$ --- $0.29\%$. Every other operator is $1\times1$ and has no
neighbours to miss. This is why the exact remedy is affordable: haloing or
coupling only the depthwise costs $+0.032\%$ of the decode at $256$\,px tiles,
against $1.745\times$ for a halo carried through the whole block, which an
earlier iteration of this work measured and rejected --- correctly about the
number and wrongly about the target.

\paragraph{The seam grows quadratically in per-tile depth, and the area law does
not describe it.} Sweeping the split depth $j$ sweeps the number of per-tile
blocks $b = (K-j)\,b_{\mathrm{exit}}$ from 12 to 0, and the pure seam penalty
follows (untrained warm start, every tile at full depth, 12 sequences):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
$b$ & area $1-(\frac{F-2b}{F})^2$ & $q0$ & $q32$ & $q63$ \\
\midrule
12 & 0.938 & 0.175 & 0.314 & 0.606 \\
10 & 0.859 & 0.100 & 0.149 & 0.234 \\
 8 & 0.750 & 0.065 & 0.107 & 0.194 \\
 6 & 0.609 & 0.032 & 0.053 & 0.089 \\
 4 & 0.438 & 0.009 & 0.018 & 0.038 \\
 2 & 0.234 & 0.003 & 0.006 & 0.017 \\
 0 & 0.000 & \textbf{0.000} & \textbf{0.000} & \textbf{0.000} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The $b=0$ row is the control and it is exactly zero at all three rates, so the
measurement is of the seam and nothing else.

The area fraction is a poor predictor: fitted with one free scale it is wrong by
160--264\% on average. It counts whether a pixel is \emph{reached} by the border
and not how far the border propagates into it, and it saturates --- at $b=12$
almost every pixel is already contaminated, yet the penalty keeps rising
steeply. A power law fits well at every rate,
\begin{equation}
\text{seam} \;\propto\; b^{\alpha}, \qquad
\alpha = 2.38,\, 2.22,\, 1.93 \text{ at } q0,\, q32,\, q63,
\end{equation}
with $r = 0.98$--$0.995$ in log--log and $12$--$22\%$ mean relative error. The
exponent near two has a reading: the number of contaminated pixels grows like the
perimeter times the depth, $\propto b$, and the error accumulated in each of them
grows with the number of convolutions that reached it, also $\propto b$. Once
every pixel is contaminated only the second factor is left, which is exactly
where the area law stops being able to say anything.

\paragraph{The repair module's ceiling.} Splitting per-pixel squared error by
distance from the nearest tile boundary at $q63$, with repair on against off and
the same exit map:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Band (px) & Share & OFF & Change \\
\midrule
0--4    & 6.2\%  & $4.7\times10^{-5}$ & $-0.27\%$ \\
4--16   & 17.3\% & $4.4\times10^{-5}$ & $+0.05\%$ \\
16--64  & 51.6\% & $4.3\times10^{-5}$ & $+0.04\%$ \\
64--128 & 25.0\% & $4.1\times10^{-5}$ & $+0.04\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The error is $15\%$ higher on the boundary band than in the interior and falls
monotonically with distance, so the artefact is real and local. But the gate
leaks: the module gains only in the first band. Taking the boundary gain and
assuming a perfect gate elsewhere bounds what it could ever earn at
$0.062 \times 4.7\!\times\!10^{-5} \times 0.0027 / 4.30\!\times\!10^{-5}
\approx 1.8\times10^{-4}$ of the frame's error, i.e. $\approx 0.0008\dB$, for
$0.95\%$ of the decode.

\section{Reproducibility}

\paragraph{Frozen encoder.} Every evaluation asserts
$\max|\theta^{\mathrm{enc}}_{\mathrm{ours}} - \theta^{\mathrm{enc}}_{\mathrm{rel}}| = 0$
before measuring. Without it the comparison is not on the same latent and the
claim of an unchanged bitstream is unverified.

\paragraph{Warm-start equivalence.} At initialisation the deepest exit
reproduces the released decoder bit-exactly at $q0/32/63$, because every
inherited weight is copied and every new module's last layer is zero.

\paragraph{Sorted execution.} The reordering of
Section~5 is bit-identical on CUDA under a random exit map and random content.
It is \emph{not} bit-identical on CPU, and the reason is not the algorithm: a
plain \texttt{DepthConvBlock} is already order-dependent at batch size 13 there,
by about $10^{-7}$, while exact at 4, 9, 16 and 40, because oneDNN selects a
different blocking. A test that compared the two paths on a decode of an
all-zero image would pass vacuously --- every tile carries the same latent, so
any permutation is trivially exact --- and ours therefore uses random content.

\paragraph{Two decibel conventions.} Pooling every tile of every frame into one
MSE and averaging a per-frame decibel differ by $0.023$--$0.033\dB$ on an
identical allocation, a quarter to a third of the working budget, with pooling
always the flattering one. We report per-frame, which is what the reference
implementation of the baseline computes.

\paragraph{Two quality metrics.} An RGB MSE ratio and the $6{:}1{:}1$-weighted
YUV 4:2:0 PSNR the baseline reports agree within $0.003\dB$ on identical
decodes across the whole rate range; we verified this rather than assuming it.

\paragraph{Timing.} \texttt{torch.cuda.Event} is created on the process's
current device, so a timing harness on a non-default GPU measures nothing in
particular and says nothing about it. Every wall-clock figure here was
re-measured with the device pinned, and a test asserts that every script using
CUDA events pins it.

\paragraph{No number in the paper is typed.} Every table and every inline
figure is generated from \texttt{results/} by one script, expanded as a macro,
and a second script re-reads \NumClaims\ of them out of the prose and compares
them against the same files, exiting non-zero on any disagreement. It has caught
four drifts, one of which was a figure count that changed when a figure was
inserted in the middle.

\section{Claims this work made and then measured}

Five statements that appeared in earlier drafts of this work, and what the
experiment said. They are collected because the pattern matters more than any one
of them: each was plausible, argued from a mechanism, and wrong in a way only a
measurement could show.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
\item \textbf{``The corrupted-area fraction explains why split depth matters.''}
      It predicts the seam with $160$--$264\%$ error, because the area
      saturates once every pixel is contaminated and the damage does not. What
      replaces it is a power law in $b$ with the exponent \emph{fitted} ---
      $2.38$, $2.22$, $1.93$ at $q0$, $q32$, $q63$ --- which fits to
      $12$--$22\%$. A fixed square, which earlier drafts quoted as though it
      were the same thing, fits to $31$--$38\%$: better than the area by a
      factor of five and worse than the fitted exponent by a factor of two.
\item \textbf{``Canvas coupling is the clearest remaining gain, worth
      $3.5$--$5$ points.''} It removes $47$--$91\%$ of the floor and collapses the
      routed saving --- \CoupPaddedMid\% to \CoupCoupledMid\% at $q32$,
      \CoupPaddedHigh\% to \CoupCoupledHigh\% at
      $q63$. Coupling is exact at uniform depth and routing is the deliberate
      violation of that condition.
\item \textbf{``A tile size chosen per resolution is the obvious extension.''}
      $128$\,px tiles help only at $416{\times}240$ ($+2.4$ points) and cost
      $1.7$--$3.3$ points elsewhere, including at $832{\times}480$ where the tile
      count rises from 8 to 28 and the saving still falls.
\item \textbf{``Configuration A costs the encoder about $1.21$ decodes.''} The
      exact search costs $4.6$. The $1.21$ figure was the full-frame table, which
      may be used to rank but not to report.
\item \textbf{``The recovery curve of partial signalling \emph{is} the Lorenz
      curve of the per-tile regret.''} It is neither an identity nor, as the
      second version of this claim said, an upper bound. Overriding a set
      removes exactly its regret at a \emph{fixed} multiplier, but the
      constraint is distortion, and returning to the budget re-bisects
      $\lambda$. That re-bisection is not a leak in the bound, it is a
      different and larger feasible set: the hybrid runs the oracle's $\lambda$
      on the overridden tiles and the router's fixed $\beta$ on the rest, and
      two multipliers reach allocations one cannot. Measured, the recovery
      exceeds the Lorenz prediction wherever that matters ---
      \LorenzTightMin--\LorenzTightMax\% of it across the sweep --- and at
      \HybridBeatsAN\ of \HybridBeatsAOf\ rates a half map beats a complete one
      outright. The separability argument was correct about the wrong quantity,
      twice.
\end{itemize}

\section{Negative results}

We list these because they cost real time and are not otherwise recoverable from
the paper.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{First-order border extrapolation.} Continuing the local gradient
      past a tile boundary is $1.8\times$ worse than a zeroth-order hold at
      $q63$ (\SeamLinearHigh$\dB$ against \SeamReplHigh). Amplifying boundary
      noise costs more than the trend recovers.
\item \textbf{Fitted AR(1) padding}~\cite{arls}. The best estimator we measured,
      and rejected: $0.02\dB$ for $10.7\%$ of decode wall-clock.
\item \textbf{Learned seam repair.} Bounded above at $\approx 0.0008\dB$ for
      $0.95\%$ of decode, even with a perfect gate.
\item \textbf{Jointly training the router with the decoder.} In one
      configuration the router collapsed to a rate-dependent constant with zero
      agreement with the oracle; in another it did not. Training the router
      against a frozen decoder is reliable and is what we report.
\item \textbf{A halo through the per-tile trunk.} $1.745\times$ the cost of a
      full decode --- more than routing saves.
\item \textbf{A rank-1 surrogate for the per-tile distortion.} Modelling
      $\log D(t,k)\approx\alpha\log b(t)+c+\log\varphi_k$ from the tile's bit
      count gives a routing rule with no learned parameters, and it works ---
      but only because it places tiles on the ladder by \emph{level}. What
      decides a tile is the \emph{spread} across the ladder, which a rank-1
      model in the level cannot represent, and the two are not the same
      ordering: bits correlate with distortion at every exit and with the
      depth the oracle assigns, at $\rho\approx0.53$, but a level-only model
      cannot see which tiles have a steep ladder and which have a flat one.
\item \textbf{A single router across all rates.} It comes within \GapMin\
      points of the oracle at $q\GapMinQp$, where the tilt the bisection has to
      apply is essentially zero because that is where its training $\lambda$
      lands, and falls to \GapMax\ at the far end. The gap tracks $|\beta|$ in
      both directions. A router per operating point, not a better architecture.
\end{itemize}

\section{Things that were not running}

Four checks this work cites were not measuring what they claimed, and all four
were found by running them rather than by reading them.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item The bit-exactness test for the warm start declared \texttt{qp} as a bare
      parameter with no \texttt{parametrize}, so \texttt{pytest} looked for a
      fixture of that name and errored at setup. It has been cited by name for
      the claim that the deepest exit reproduces the released decoder exactly.
      Parametrised over three rates and every warm start on disk: six cases,
      all exact.
\item The equality check for the sorted per-tile loop compared the two paths on a
      decode of \texttt{torch.zeros}, where every tile carries the same latent
      and any permutation is trivially exact. On random content with a mixed exit
      map the result is still bit-identical on CUDA, but the original check could
      not have failed.
\item Every wall-clock measurement in this work was taken with
      \texttt{torch.cuda.Event}, which is created on the process's \emph{current}
      device rather than on the device the tensors live on. The timing runs used
      a non-default GPU, so the events and the work were on different cards.
      This does not raise: it returns numbers. The tell, in hindsight, was that
      the released decoder appeared to take $274$, $401$ and $512$\,ms at $q0$,
      $q32$ and $q63$ --- a fixed-architecture synthesis network whose
      arithmetic does not depend on the quality index cannot get slower with
      it. Re-measured with the device pinned it is $111$\,ms at all three. The
      corrections are not small and one of them reverses a claim: the tiling
      overhead falls from $8.5\%$ to \TilingOverhead\%, and the unsorted
      per-tile loop, reported as \emph{slower} than the dense decoder at high
      rate, in fact realises \WallMasked\% at $q0$ --- so sorting the tiles is
      a $\WallSortedGain$-point improvement on something that already worked
      rather than a rescue of something that did not. The wall-clock section of
      the main paper is written against the corrected numbers. Anything in this
      repository that timed a non-default GPU should be assumed to have had this
      bug; a source test now asserts that every script using
      \texttt{torch.cuda.Event} pins the device first, and it found two more.
\item The router head suppresses exits below the split depth by assigning them
      $-10^{4}$. That is a mask only while the head's own logits stay well above
      it, and this head's did not: nothing in a cross-entropy or a regret
      objective penalises a common offset, one drifted in during training, and
      the raw outputs settled near $-10^{4}$. The two suppressed entries then
      became the \emph{largest} in every row, took nearly all the probability
      mass, and the caller's clamp turned the resulting choice into the cheapest
      real exit --- so a large share of every configuration-B allocation went to
      the cheapest rung for a reason unrelated to the tile. The mask is now
      $-\infty$, which cannot drift, and a test checks the property across six
      logit scales rather than checking the constant.
\end{itemize}
